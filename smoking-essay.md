# Health Impacts and Diseases Caused by Smoking

Smoking remains one of the largest preventable threats to human health. The World Health Organization estimates that about 1.3 billion people smoke and that tobacco causes roughly 8 million deaths each year.¹ Smoking is often linked only with lung disease, but it harms almost every organ system and also affects people who do not smoke.²

The respiratory system is exposed to smoke first. Cigarette smoke carries thousands of chemicals, including oxidants and free radicals, that can overwhelm the body's antioxidant defences.¹ Over time this causes chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), emphysema, bronchial asthma, lung fibrosis, interstitial lung disease and lung cancer.² Almost every smoker develops some degree of respiratory bronchiolitis, and smoking is a major risk factor for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Tar and toxins build up in the lungs and, after years of exposure, permanently change their structure.²

Smoking also damages the heart and blood vessels. Nicotine speeds up atherosclerosis, the build-up of fatty plaque in artery walls, which leads to coronary heart disease.² Atherosclerosis is also the main cause of ischaemic stroke and peripheral artery disease.¹ COPD and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease are closely linked, and both share the same smoking-driven mechanisms of systemic inflammation, oxidative stress and damage to the endothelium, the inner lining of blood vessels.¹ Up to 80% of patients with peripheral artery disease are current or former smokers.¹ It makes high blood pressure worse and can push it toward severe forms such as malignant hypertension.²

Reproduction and pregnancy are affected too. In men it is linked to erectile dysfunction and hormonal imbalance, and the more a man smokes, the higher his risk. In women it disrupts hormones and fertility. During pregnancy it restricts fetal growth, which leads to smaller head size, shorter femur length and low birth weight.²

Smoking affects other organs as well. It weakens the immune system, upsets the normal bacteria of the mouth and throat, and makes people more vulnerable to infections such as oral candidiasis.² It worsens gum disease, slows the healing of broken bones and raises the rate of infection after surgery. It is also linked to dry eye, reduced blood-vessel density in the optic nerve of glaucoma patients, weaker inner-ear (otoacoustic) responses and a higher risk of Alzheimer's disease.²

Second-hand smoke harms people who do not smoke. Passive smokers show raised markers of cardiovascular risk, such as C-reactive protein and fibrinogen. The cardiovascular effects of second-hand smoke appear quickly and come close to those of active smoking.² Hookah and waterpipe smoke contain many of the same toxicants as cigarettes, and their effects on the heart are comparable. E-cigarettes also harm the respiratory health of teenagers with asthma.²

Stopping smoking gives strong protection against many of these diseases.² Some of the damage can be reversed. After a person stops, the normal bacteria of the mouth return and gum disease responds better to treatment.² Clear public-health campaigns, enforcement of smoke-free laws and access to counselling for quitting can protect smokers and the people around them.²
