# The Hidden Cost of Every Cigarette: How Smoking Damages the Body

Smoking remains one of the largest preventable threats to human health. The World Health Organization estimates that about 1.3 billion people smoke and that tobacco causes roughly 8 million deaths each year (Kotlyarov, 2023). Most people know smoking harms the lungs. Fewer realise that it damages almost every organ system, and that it also hurts people who never light a cigarette.

The respiratory system takes the most direct damage. Cigarette smoke carries thousands of chemicals, including oxidants and free radicals, that can overwhelm the body's antioxidant defences (Kotlyarov, 2023). Over time this causes chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), emphysema, bronchial asthma, lung fibrosis, interstitial lung disease and lung cancer (Varghese & Muntode Gharde, 2023). Almost every smoker develops some degree of respiratory bronchiolitis, and smoking is a major risk factor for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Tar and toxins build up in the lungs and, after years of exposure, permanently change their structure.

The heart and blood vessels are harmed just as seriously. Nicotine speeds up atherosclerosis, the build-up of fatty plaque in artery walls, which leads to coronary heart disease, stroke and peripheral artery disease (Varghese & Muntode Gharde, 2023). Kotlyarov (2023) shows that COPD and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease are closely linked. Both diseases share the same smoking-driven mechanisms: systemic inflammation, oxidative stress and damage to the endothelium, the inner lining of blood vessels. Up to 80% of patients with peripheral artery disease are current or former smokers. Smoking also makes high blood pressure worse and can push it toward its most dangerous forms.

Smoking also affects reproduction and the next generation. In men it is linked to erectile dysfunction and hormonal imbalance, and the more a man smokes, the higher his risk. In women it disrupts hormones and fertility. During pregnancy it restricts fetal growth, which leads to smaller head size, shorter femur length and low birth weight (Varghese & Muntode Gharde, 2023).

Other effects are often overlooked. Smoking weakens the immune system, upsets the normal bacteria of the mouth and throat, and makes people more vulnerable to infections such as oral candidiasis. It worsens gum disease, slows the healing of broken bones and raises the rate of infection after surgery. It is also linked to dry eye, glaucoma progression, reduced hearing function and a higher risk of Alzheimer's disease (Varghese & Muntode Gharde, 2023).

Second-hand smoke spreads this harm to others. Passive smokers show raised markers of cardiovascular risk, such as C-reactive protein and fibrinogen. The cardiovascular effects of second-hand smoke appear quickly and come close to those of active smoking. Newer products are no safe escape either. Hookah and waterpipe smoke contain many of the same toxicants as cigarettes and harm the heart just as much. E-cigarettes also damage respiratory health, especially in teenagers with asthma (Varghese & Muntode Gharde, 2023).

Because smoking harms so many organs, quitting is one of the most powerful steps a person can take for their health. Some of the damage can be reversed: the bacteria of the mouth return to normal and gum disease responds better to treatment after a person stops. Stronger public-health campaigns, firm enforcement of smoke-free laws and accessible cessation support can protect smokers and the people around them.
